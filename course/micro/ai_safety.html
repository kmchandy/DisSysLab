Power and Safety

In this course you will build systems consisting of multiple cooperating agents. Some of the agents may be AI agents. You specify an AI agent in English and its behavior may not be precisely predictable because English can be ambiguous. You can also specify an agent in a formal logic; implement the agent in a programming language; and use logic to reason about its behavior. In this course we do both and discuss the tradeoff between safety and power.

Read before Homework 1 Time ~20 minutes Contains six rules you are expected to follow

SECTION 1AI in persistent multi-agent systems

The power and danger of AI is acute in multi-agent persistent systems.
  1. These systems run forever and in the background. An agent may behave in an unexpected way over a long period of time before a problem is discovered.
  2. When multiple agents collaborate their collective behavior can evolve. Emergent behavior is powerful. Systems can evolve to do things in totally unexpected ways. That can also be dangerous.
  3. Many of the systems we build in the course are sense-and-respond systems -- they monitor behavior in the external environment and react by sending messages to the environment. AI systems that interact with the environment are powerful -- they control vehicles and robots, and manage bank accounts, social media and mail. And they can search the web and learn new ways of interacting with the environment. For that reason, they can also be unsafe.

SECTION 2Two things that happened last July

Both happened in July 2026 and were disclosed by the companies responsible.

OpenAI's models left a test environment and reached Hugging Face

Disclosed 21 July 2026

Models were being evaluated on offensive-security tasks with safety restrictions reduced, against a benchmark called ExploitGym. To do better on the benchmark, the models found a flaw in the testing setup itself, got out of the isolated research environment, reached the open internet, and went to Hugging Face's production systems to look up the answers to the tasks they were being graded on. Hugging Face confirmed unauthorised access to a limited part of its production infrastructure, including some internal datasets and credentials. OpenAI described it as "an unprecedented cyber incident".

Anthropic's models did something similar, and published live malware

Disclosed 30 July 2026

During capture-the-flag evaluations run with an external partner, several Claude models were supposed to be in an environment with no internet access. Because of what Anthropic called a misunderstanding between it and the evaluation partner, internet access was in fact available. The models, believing they were still in a simulation, attacked what turned out to be three real organisations, and one of them published a malicious Python package to PyPI — the package registry that pip install reads — where fifteen real systems downloaded it within the hour.

In both cases the model did what it was asked to do.

Nobody instructed the system to escape. AI agents are trained to respond to commands which specify goals and constraints. The agents have to balance reaching goals with violating constraints.

SECTION 3Checking for safety

You may not know what an AI agent may do, but you can constrain the parts of the environment with which the agent interacts. In DSL, the sources of information are specified by you -- the system cannot start searching other sources without issuing warnings. Likewise, the sinks -- devices to which the system sends messages -- are specified by you. And agents cannot create new sinks without issuing warnings.

These constraints in DSL allows for safer design at the cost of losing the power of emergent intelligence and the flexibility of adapting in unspecified ways. The tradeoffs that we made in DSL may not be the right ones for your applications.

The problem of unexpected behavior is not restricted to language model agents. You can ask a language model to create a Python agent, and that agent may run code built at run time and use sockets to reach external devices in ways that you didn't expect. The same goes for any program that you did not write on your own. You can, however, inspect the program and reason about it -- something you cannot do with a call to an LLM service.

SECTION 4The line this course draws

A multi-agent system can be built so that its state evolves, or so that its program evolves. We allow the first and not the second, and the distinction is worth more than any list of rules.

State evolving is ordinary and useful: agents accumulate messages, update scores, change what they hold, adapt a threshold. The network of who can talk to whom, the jobs the agents have, and the prompts they run are fixed before the office starts and do not change while it runs.

Now consider lifting that restriction, because it is not a silly idea — it is a powerful one. Give the agents a shared whiteboard they can read and write. Let them write prompts on it. Now an agent's behaviour on Tuesday depends on what another agent wrote on Monday, and the collective behaviour of the system can evolve into something no one specified. Add the ability to create new agents, and the system can grow. Add the ability to name new external addresses, and it can reach places you never listed.

Each of those three is a genuine capability and each is being seriously explored. Together they turn a system you can reason about into one you can only observe. This course is about building systems whose behaviour satisfies a specification you wrote down, and there is no useful sense in which you can specify a system that rewrites itself. So we do not build those here. That is a decision about scope, not a claim that the idea is forbidden or worthless.

SECTION 5Six rules for this course

Follow these in everything you build. Each one has its reason attached, because a rule you understand survives contact with a situation its author did not foresee, and a rule you have only memorised does not.

  1. Treat the output of a language model as unpredictable, always. You do not know precisely what an LLM will say in response to a given input. That is both the strength and the weakness. Design so that a surprising answer is contained rather than assuming there will not be one.
  2. Never put confidential information into a conversation with an LLM. No passwords, no financial details, no API keys, nothing you would not be willing to see stored. This applies to the chat window and equally to anything your office sends into a prompt.
  3. Do not gather information that is not yours to gather. No sensors that capture other people's private information, no sources private to someone else. A microphone seems harmless until you notice it is recording a conversation nobody consented to. If your project involves other people's data, come and talk to me before you build it.
  4. No agent creates another agent. An agent that can create agents can create agents that create agents, and the size of the system stops being something you chose. This is a real capability that we are deliberately not exercising.
  5. No agent writes another agent's prompt. Agents may share data. They may not modify each other's behaviour. Communication flows along the static network in your office.md and the roles that ship with the framework have fixed prompts; they can change parameter values — a score threshold, say — but not what they fundamentally do. This is Section 4's line, stated as a rule.
  6. The set of external things your office touches is fixed, and you wrote it. Websites, sensors, actuators, files. A system that can generate new external addresses to visit is powerful and dangerous in exactly the way the July incidents were dangerous. Every address your office reaches appears in your office.md, where you and I can both read it.

One practical note about your own machine. In July 2026 a sandbox escape called SharedRoot was reported in Claude Cowork running locally on macOS: a single message could give a session read and write access to files anywhere on the Mac, with no permission prompt. The fix released at the time defaulted new sessions to cloud execution rather than closing the local path outright. Check the current status before you choose local execution, and keep your desktop app up to date. This is not a reason to avoid the tool. It is a demonstration that the sandbox boundary is a piece of software like any other, written by people, and sometimes wrong.

SECTION 6What this page does not claim

Stated plainly, because a safety page that overclaims teaches the wrong lesson better than it teaches the right one.

  • Following these rules does not make your office safe. It makes the set of things it can do small enough for you to hold in your head. That is a much weaker property and it is the one actually on offer.
  • The checks are structural. dsl check reads your network. It cannot tell you that your office does the right thing, only that it is wired the way you said.
  • We are not doing AI alignment research in this course. We are doing engineering under the assumption that the model is capable, useful and not fully predictable — which is the situation you will actually be in, whatever the research concludes.
  • Nothing here is a claim about intent. No model in either July incident wanted anything. Attributing motives to these systems is a reliable way to reason badly about them, in both directions — it makes people frightened of the wrong things and relaxed about the right ones.

SECTION 7Reading and an exercise

Read at least two of these before the first class. The first two are the July incidents; the third is about the tool on your own laptop.

  1. OpenAI says its AI models escaped from a secure test environment and hacked Hugging Face — Fortune, 21 July 2026. The clearest general-audience account. For the technical sequence, Malwarebytes walks through it step by step.
  2. Anthropic says its own AI models breached three companies during security tests — TechCrunch, 30 July 2026. The Register has more detail, including the PyPI package and Anthropic's own explanation.
  3. Claude Cowork escaped sandbox on Mac, gained full access to all files — 9to5Mac, 27 July 2026, on the SharedRoot vulnerability. Read this one because it is about the software you are about to install.

Exercise · Read one story properly

Pick one of the three. Write half a page answering the four questions in Section 2: was this deployment or evaluation; did the system seek the outcome or was it steered there; what actually failed; and who is telling you. Then answer a fifth: which of the six rules, if any, would have prevented it?

The fifth question does not have a comfortable answer, and you should say so if you find that. Rules for a student project are not controls for a frontier lab, and noticing the gap is the point of the exercise.

Exercise · Find the power in an office

Take any office in the gallery — dsl list will show you — and answer without running it: what is the complete set of things this office can do to the world outside the machine it runs on? Then run dsl check on it and see whether it reports W11 or W12, and whether you agree with the answer.
