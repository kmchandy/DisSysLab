Byzantine Generals Consensus

Consensus when agents may lie

The Byzantine generals consensus algorithm is a consensus algorithm specified by the requirements of validity, agreement and termination see lecture_07.html The Byzantine algorithm allows non-faulty agents to reach a consensus even if faulty agents halt, lie and forward modified messages. The FLP theorem says consensus cannot be reached in this case. Byzantine consensus makes an assumption of synchrony for which FLP does not apply.

The assumption of synchronous rounds Byzantine consensus algorithms are synchronous. The algorithm proceeds in rounds: every message sent by a non-faulty agent to a non-faulty agent in one round is delivered before the next round begins.

How a round is implemented is left unspecified. If message delays are under t seconds and clocks are synchronized, round n can be implemented by having every agent send its messages at time n·T for some T > t, and treating every message that arrives in [n·T, (n+1)·T) as belonging to round n.

Specification

The consensus problem is to come to consensus on a value. We will assume that the value is a boolean or a single bit. To get consensus on a 64-bit value, the brute-force way is to run the algorithm 64 times, once for each bit; however, there are more efficient ways, which we won't discuss here.

Some computer scientists have a (wierd?) sense of humor. In Leslie Lamport's story, Paxos is a Greek island where voting requirements gave rise to a consensus algorithm. Later we will look at the Dining Philosophers Problem and the Drinking Philosophers Problem which have nothing to do with philosophers, dining or drinking. The Byzantine Generals Problem is a story about an army with a general and officers where the general and some officers may be traitors. The problem is to develop an algorithm by which loyal officers can come to a consensus on whether to attack or retreat, despite the traitors.

There is one general and N lieutenants. The general gives a command, which is either retreat (0) or attack (1).

  • A loyal general gives the same command to every lieutenant.
  • A disloyal general may give different commands to different lieutenants, and may give no command at all to some of them.
  • Loyal lieutenants obey the protocol. Disloyal lieutenants may do anything.

You are given t, an upper bound on the number of disloyal officers — and the general is one of the officers it counts. Nobody knows which officers are disloyal. Your task is to design a consensus protocol that has the following specification.

The specification

  1. Termination The algorithm terminates
  2. Agreement Loyal lieutenants come to a consensus. When the algorithm terminates, all loyal lieutenants have come to a consensus to attack or all loyal lieutenants have come to a consensus to retreat. retreat.
  3. Validity Loyal lieutenants obey a loyal general.

The specification does not require that disloyal officers be identified. If the specification does not have the validity requirement, then the solution is trivial: all loyal lieutenants retreat. If the specification does not have the agreement requirement, then the solution is trivial: obey the general. The problem is non-trivial because both requirements must be satisfied.

Example 1: General and five lieutenants Let's look at the case where the general and one of the five lieutenants is disloyal. The general sends attack messages to two of the loyal lieutenans, and retreat messages to the other two loyal lieutenants. The disloyal lieutenant is an adversary that sends messages to the loyal lieutenants to break consensus.

Suppose the lieutenants decide to take a majority vote. Two vote attack and two vote retreat. The disloyal lieutenant can swing the decision made by each of the loyal lieutenants.

Now consider the case of a disloyal general and a million loyal lieutenants. The disloyal general can send attack messages to 500,000 loyal lieutenants and 499,999 retreate messages to the other loyal lieutenants. The disloyal lieutenant can swing a majority vote either way.

Why anyone cares

The name is a story, and the story is unfair to Byzantine officers, who were no more treacherous than anyone else’s. Read the general as a fire detector that may be faulty, and the lieutenants as the agents that must decide together whether to sound the alarm.

Validity then says: if a working detector reports a fire, every working agent raises the alarm. Consensus says: even if the detector is faulty and tells some agents there is a fire and others that there is none, the working agents still agree — either they all raise the alarm or none of them do. What we will not accept is some of them ringing while the others stay quiet.

Two kinds of message

Everything turns on what a traitor can do to a letter it has been given.

  1. Written Written, or encrypted, messages. A letter containing message M signed by agent X and received by agent Y can be forwarded by Y, but Y can neither change the message nor forge X’s signature.
  2. Oral Oral, or unencrypted, messages. An agent can forge signatures and corrupt messages.

The rest of this lecture is the written case. Oral messages come next, and they cost more: with oral messages no algorithm works unless more than two thirds of the officers are loyal.

The algorithm, with signed messages

A lieutenant that has not committed to attack by the end of the algorithm commits to retreat. So the whole algorithm is a rule for when to commit to attack, and the letters are the evidence for doing it.

Round 0

The general signs one message — attack or retreat — and sends it to each lieutenant. Then the general plays no further part. A lieutenant that receives no message from the general in round 0 behaves exactly as though it had received retreat.

Round j, for j > 0

The rule A lieutenant L that has not committed to attack on an earlier round, and that holds a signed attack message from the general and signed attack messages from at least j−1 distinct lieutenants other than L itself, commits to attack on round j. It then sends every lieutenant copies of that evidence — the general’s letter and the j−1 lieutenants’ letters — together with an attack message signed by L itself.

On round 1 that reduces to: a loyal lieutenant commits to attack if it received an attack message from the general, because j−1 = 0 letters from other lieutenants are required.

Three observations

  1. If the general is loyal and sends attack messages, then every loyal lieutenant commits to attack in round 1. Why On round 1 the rule asks for the general’s letter and nothing else, and a loyal general sent that letter to everyone.
  2. If the general is loyal and never sends an attack message, then no loyal lieutenant ever commits to attack, in any round. Why A lieutenant commits to attack only if it holds a signed attack message from the general. A loyal general signed none, and under the written model nobody can forge the general’s signature.
  3. If a loyal lieutenant commits to attack on round j, then every loyal lieutenant commits to attack by round j+1. Why L committed on round j, so it broadcast the general’s letter, the letters of j−1 other lieutenants, and its own. Every lieutenant therefore starts round j+1 holding the general’s letter and attack messages signed by j distinct lieutenants — which is exactly what the rule asks for on round j+1.

So which round can the algorithm stop on?

Observations 1 and 2 settle a loyal general completely: round 1 decides it, and nothing later can change it. The question is what a disloyal general can drag out, and the examples are the way to see it. In both of the runs below there are five lieutenants, A, B, C loyal and Y, Z disloyal, and the general is disloyal too.

Run 2 is the whole difficulty in miniature. A disloyal general and two disloyal lieutenants can delay the commit to round 3 and the consensus to round 4. The question is whether they can keep doing it for ever.

Theorem

Let t be an upper bound on the number of disloyal officers, the general included. If no loyal lieutenant commits to attack on rounds 1, …, t, then no loyal lieutenant commits to attack on any later round.

Proof

By observations 1 and 2, a loyal general settles the matter in round 1, so only a disloyal general is left to consider. The general is then one of the at most t disloyal officers, so at most t−1 lieutenants are disloyal.

Suppose no loyal lieutenant has committed to attack in rounds 1, …, t. Then on round t+1 the only attack messages a loyal lieutenant can be holding from other lieutenants are the ones signed by those at most t−1 traitors — no loyal lieutenant has sent one, because none has committed.

But the rule for round t+1 asks for signatures from t distinct lieutenants. There are not that many available. So no loyal lieutenant commits on round t+1, and the same count rules out every round after it.

The answer The algorithm can stop after round t+1. Every loyal lieutenant that has not committed to attack by then commits to retreat, and by observation 3 they are all in the same position.

What comes next

All of this rested on the seal: a traitor could forward a letter but not change it, and the general’s signature could not be forged. Take the seal away — oral messages — and a traitor can say “the general told me to attack” when the general said nothing at all. That is the next lecture, and the first thing it costs us is a bound: there is no solution when the number of loyal agents is at most twice the number of disloyal ones.
